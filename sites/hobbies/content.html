Advice for International Students

What I would prepare differently for studying and building a career in America. These are personal suggestions; your goals and budget may differ.

1. Choose a degree with a career plan

If your goal is working in the U.S., I would consider a STEM-eligible program that fits your interests and target jobs. Check the exact program’s STEM OPT eligibility with the school, and plan for OPT and possible employer-sponsored H-1B early. Eligible students can apply for a 24-month STEM OPT extension after the initial post-completion OPT period of up to 12 months, allowing up to 36 months in total if approved. Those two extra years can give a sponsoring employer more opportunities to register you for the annual H-1B selection process (lottery), depending on timing and continued eligibility; selection is not guaranteed. Neither a degree nor STEM OPT guarantees a job or long-term status.

2. Plan the full path for a theoretical degree

With a theoretical subject such as pure mathematics, I would plan beyond the degree title. Proof-based study can involve a long learning curve before you are ready for independent research, and an undergraduate degree alone may not prepare you for a specific job. Look at actual entry-level roles and the coding, statistics, applied knowledge, or experience they require. Do not assume the degree by itself will lead to U.S. employment or sponsorship.

Before committing, decide whether you want to work after your bachelor’s or pursue a master’s or PhD, and whether you are prepared for the academic demands and extra years. For an academic research career, plan for doctoral study. Work out the full cost: tuition, living expenses, funding conditions, and income you would forgo. Keep an alternative plan if funding, admission, or your interests change.

One route worth comparing is an affordable undergraduate degree outside the U.S., followed by a U.S. master’s. I would compare specific programs in Japan, Taiwan, the UK, or Australia, including the option of studying at home. None is automatically cheaper: compare total costs, scholarships, course length, language requirements, and whether the degree meets the U.S. graduate program’s admissions prerequisites.

3. Be clear about why you are going

A year of cultural exchange can be worthwhile on its own. If learning English is the main goal, I would compare options in the UK and Australia before paying for a U.S. program. Compare tuition, housing and course length rather than assume one country is always cheaper. If building an overseas career is the goal, research the programs, job market and work-authorization requirements before enrolling.

4. Start preparing before you arrive

I would build practical coding skills before university—perhaps through an affordable short coding bootcamp at home, even for physics, chemistry or math. Compare cost and quality; a certificate is not a degree or job guarantee. Open LinkedIn early, build projects, meet people and learn internship recruiting timelines instead of waiting until graduation.
